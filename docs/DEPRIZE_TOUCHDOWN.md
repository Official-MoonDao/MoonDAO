# PART I — ONE PAGER

## 1. The question

> **Which organization will next put a spacecraft on the Moon, upright, and keep it
> working for a day?**

Not the first landing of the decade — that already happened. Not "who will try." The
next one that *works*.

## 2. Why this prize, and why now

Between January 2024 and June 2025 the world attempted seven lunar landings. Two fully
succeeded. Two landed and immediately fell over. Three never made it to a working
surface. The commercial Moon is no longer theoretical, and it is also not reliable.

| Date | Mission | Operator | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jan 2024 | Peregrine Mission One | Astrobotic | Failed in Earth orbit. Propellant leak. Never left for the Moon |
| Jan 2024 | SLIM | JAXA | Landed, on its side. Partial. Japan’s first |
| Feb 2024 | IM-1 Odysseus | Intuitive Machines | Soft landing, snapped a leg, tipped. First commercial touchdown. Operated ~7 days on its side |
| Jun 2024 | Chang'e-6 | CNSA | Full success. Far-side sample return |
| Mar 2, 2025 | **Blue Ghost Mission 1** | **Firefly Aerospace** | **Full success.** Upright at Mare Crisium. Full 14-day surface mission. First fully successful commercial landing |
| Mar 2025 | IM-2 Athena | Intuitive Machines | Soft touchdown near the south pole, tipped into a crater. Partial failure. ~250 MB of NASA data |
| Jun 5–6, 2025 | Hakuto-R Mission 2 / Resilience | ispace | Lost telemetry ~90 s before touchdown. Hard landing. Laser rangefinder anomaly |

As of 4 September 2026, **nobody has landed since Firefly**. Chang'e-7 was on the pad
in August and was stood down. The next twelve to eighteen months contain the densest
cluster of landing attempts since Apollo. A market that prices *who actually makes it*
is pricing something the industry itself cannot currently forecast — the 2024–25 record
is the evidence.

# PART III — PRIZE RULES v0.1

## 1. The prize in one sentence

> **The first organization, after this market opens, to put a spacecraft on the lunar
> surface in a stable planned orientation and keep it returning data for 24 hours.**

## 2. Definitions

**Organization** — the entity that designed and operates the landing vehicle. Not the
launch provider, not a payload customer, not a host government unless that government
is also the lander operator.

Worked examples, so this does not get argued later:

| Flight | Winner, if it qualifies |
|---|---|
| Griffin-1 carrying Astrolab’s FLIP | **Astrobotic** |
| Blue Ghost 2 carrying LuSEE-Night and ESA Pathfinder | **Firefly** |
| IM-3 carrying Lunar Vertex and Altus-1 | **Intuitive Machines** |
| Endurance carrying NASA cameras | **Blue Origin** |
| Chang'e-7 lander, rover and hopper | **CNSA** |
| LUPEX / Chandrayaan-5 (ISRO lander, JAXA rover) | **ISRO** |
| Artemis III crew on a Blue Origin Mark 2 | **Blue Origin** |

**Qualifying Landing** — all of the following, in order:

1. **After open.** Touchdown UTC is after the DePrize is `OPEN`.
2. **Controlled descent.** The vehicle performs a powered descent from lunar orbit or
   from a lunar transfer, under its own propulsion, and is intended to come to rest on
   the surface. Impactors, crashed stages, and hoppers deployed *from an already-landed
   vehicle* do not independently qualify. (Chang'e-7’s hopper is part of the lander’s
   landing, not a second landing.)
3. **Stable planned orientation.** After coming to rest, the vehicle is in an
   orientation consistent with its published landing design — upright, or whatever
   attitude the operator published as nominal — and is not on its side, inverted, or
   in a crater wall. This is the clause that scores IM-1 and IM-2 as not qualifying.
4. **Twenty-four hours of surface data.** The vehicle returns telemetry or imagery from
   the lunar surface for a continuous period of ≥ 24 hours after touchdown.
5. **Independent confirmation.** At least one of: (a) a public statement by NASA, ESA,
   CNSA, JAXA, ISRO or Roscosmos confirming a successful soft landing; (b) imagery of
   the landed vehicle from a third-party spacecraft (LRO or equivalent); (c) independently
   received surface telemetry by a third party.

If any one of these fails, it is not a Qualifying Landing. The organization may try
again; the first one that clears all five wins.

## 3. Why these five tests, and what they exclude

| Historical case | Result under these rules |
|---|---|
| Firefly Blue Ghost 1, Mar 2025 | Would qualify. Upright, 14 days of surface ops, NASA confirmation |
| Chang'e-6, 2024 | Would qualify |
| Chandrayaan-3, 2023 | Would qualify |
| IM-1 Odysseus, Feb 2024 | **Would not.** Tipped. Failed test 3 |
| IM-2 Athena, Mar 2025 | **Would not.** Tipped into a crater. Failed test 3 |
| ispace Resilience, Jun 2025 | **Would not.** Hard landing, no surface ops |
| JAXA SLIM, 2024 | **Would not.** Landed on its side |
| Astrobotic Peregrine, 2024 | **Would not.** Never reached the Moon |
| A lander that touches down cleanly and dies at hour 6 | **Would not.** Failed test 4 |
| A lander that is upright but whose only evidence is the operator’s livestream | **Would not**, unless test 5 is also met. CNSA imagery plus a CNSA success declaration satisfies 5(a) |

Test 3 is the one that will be argued. It exists because “we landed” has been used, in
public, for vehicles that came to rest on their side and could not do the mission they
were paid for. Firefly’s own marketing draws exactly this distinction; the prize
adopts it.

Test 4 exists so that a vehicle which hits the surface, sends one packet, and dies does
not win. Twenty-four hours is one Earth day, not a lunar day, and is short enough that
a south-polar mission in a short illumination window can still clear it.

Test 5 exists so that nobody grades their own homework. A government agency confirming
its own landing is accepted because the alternative — refusing to score Chang'e-7
without NASA imagery — would make the prize parochial. LRO imaging of a Chinese lander
would be sufficient and is historically how Chang'e-3 and Chang'e-4 were independently
confirmed.

## 4. Ties, slips, and what does not count

**Ties.** Earlier touchdown UTC wins. If two vehicles land in the same second, earlier
launch UTC breaks the tie.

**Crewed landings count.** An Artemis crew landing is a Qualifying Landing for the
organization that operates the lander (Blue Origin Mark 2, on current contracts). It is
not next on any published timeline.

**Orbiters, impactors, and flybys do not count.** Artemis II (crewed flyby, 2026),
CAPSTONE 02, Luna-26, ispace Mission 2.5, Lunar Pathfinder, and any other non-landing
mission are irrelevant.

**A failed attempt is not a withdrawal.** Intuitive Machines can fail IM-3 and win on
IM-4. The slot is the organization, not the flight.

## 5. Resolution

No referee panel and no laboratory. The evidence is public.

MoonDAO publishes a one-page checklist against §2 at the moment any listed organization
attempts a landing. The Senate votes on that checklist. The vote is clerical: were the
five tests met, and at what UTC. If the Senate cannot fill the checklist from public
sources within 14 days of touchdown, the landing is scored as not yet qualified and the
market stays open.

Rules freeze when the market opens. Afterwards, only clarifications that cannot change
an outcome.

## 6. Roster and eligibility

Teams claim; they do not apply. Listing is editorial. The outcome set is frozen at
`prepareCondition` and can never be resized; a genuinely new contender is absorbed by
Open Field or, if field odds demand it, by `supersede` onto a new named roster.

## 7. Mechanism

Same DePrize infrastructure as Night Shift. Bettors buy outcome tokens; 5% of volume
accrues to the Juicebox pool; the Senate settles; `releaseM1` and `completeM2` fire in
the same Safe batch so the hard-coded 30/70 split is invisible.

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Purse | **$25,000**, plus 5% of betting volume |
| Outcome slots | **6** — five named + Open Field |
| Deadline | Register at 24 months, extend annually with `setSunset` until claimed |
| Resolution | Public checklist → Senate vote → Safe transaction |

This market will probably resolve in months, not years. That is the point of it, and
it is why it can sit next to Night Shift without competing for attention: Touchdown is
the live one; Night Shift is the long one.

# PART V — QUESTIONS FOR REVIEWERS

1. **Does test 3 (stable planned orientation) correctly score the last seven attempts?**
   Walk IM-1, IM-2, SLIM, Blue Ghost 1, Chang'e-6, Resilience, and Peregrine against it
   and tell us if any result feels wrong.
2. **Is 24 hours of surface data the right duration?** Too short to mean anything, or
   long enough that a polar lander in a tight illumination window is structurally
   excluded?
3. **Is a CNSA success declaration plus official imagery enough independent confirmation?**
   Or does a Chinese landing need LRO (or equivalent) imaging before we pay?
4. **Should ispace be a named slot?** They cannot be next on the published calendar, but
   they are the most famous commercial failure on the board, and a market that hides
   them in the field will be asked about them on day one.
5. **Should this resolve on landing, or on launch-plus-landing** so a vehicle that
   leaves Earth after a rival but lands first still wins? (Current rule: landing UTC
   only.)
6. **Who did we miss for 2027?** In particular any national programme, commercial
