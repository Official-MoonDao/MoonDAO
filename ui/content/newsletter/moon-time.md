## NASA Crew 6 Docked at ISS

After a few delays, including a Monday morning scrub with just about two minutes on the clock, NASA’s Crew-6 team launched aboard a Falcon 9 rocket early on Thursday morning and successfully docked with the International Space Station approximately 24 hours later, although not without a little drama.

The docking was scheduled to occur an hour earlier but they were delayed as they attempted to troubleshoot a faulty sensor with one of the 12 hooks that help the capsule connect to the ISS. SpaceX was able to upload a software override that fixed the faulty sensor and allowed the Dragon capsule to dock with the Harmony module of the ISS without incident.

With the arrival of the new international team of astronauts, there are currently 14 people in space, although Crew-5 is set to undock from the ISS on March 9th and return to Earth after a six-month stay in low earth orbit.

## Lunar Time Zone?

What time is it on the Moon? Given the growing number of missions set to return to the lunar surface in the coming years, this is a question that still needs to be resolved given the importance and urgency of defining a common lunar reference time and satellite positioning systems.

Up until now, the lunar time has just been exported from the terrestrial agency in charge of the mission, but now dozens of companies, governments, and space agencies from around the world are looking to operate from the Moon, which could create conflicting standards without cooperation.

The international community is in the process of rolling out the framework for LunaNet, a mutually agreed upon set of standards, protocols, and interface requirements in order to create something like the GPS system on earth or similar initiatives launched by different countries such as Galileo, GLONASS, or BeiDou. In order to use satellite positioning systems on the Moon, we need precise and synchronized measurements of time. But all those earthbound systems use their own time measurements which require offsets to synchronize – something that can be avoided when building out LunaNet or similar systems if we can establish a standardized lunar time.

But doing so is not necessarily easy, as there are some unique problems and considerations when trying to establish a Universal Standard Lunar Time, including the fact that a day on the Moon lasts 29.5 Earth days, clocks actually run faster on the Moon, gaining about 56 microseconds per day, and there is a subtle difference between how objects in lunar orbit keep time vs. objects on the lunar surface. The fact that time moves differently on the Moon is something that NASA engineers have been aware of for decades, dating back to the Apollo missions and having to make relativistic time corrections.

## ispace Lunar Lander

Speaking of lunar landers, Japanese space startup ispace's lunar lander reached its farthest distance from earth, and is on track for a lunar landing attempt in late April. The privately built Hakuto-R Mission 1 lunar lander was launched from Florida in December, and while it had a few hiccups on the way, none of them jeopardized the flight and everything is still on track.

If successful, this would be the first lunar landing by a private company. Only the United States, Russia, and China have successfully landed on the Moon, with recent attempts by other nations ending in failure. Astrobotics and Intuitive Machines are also working toward a lunar landing in the near future.
