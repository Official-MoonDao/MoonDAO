## Bite-Sized Space News

## JAXA Lunar Lander

Japan's Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA) successfully launched a lunar exploration spacecraft, Smart Lander for Investigating Moon (SLIM), with the goal of becoming the world's fifth country to land on the moon. The launch took place from the Tanegashima Space Center, following several postponements due to unfavorable weather conditions. Dubbed the "moon sniper," Japan's mission aims to achieve a high-precision landing within 100 meters of its lunar target site.

SLIM is expected to begin its lunar landing by February after a four month journey to lunar orbit thanks to a fuel-efficient approach trajectory. The primary objective of SLIM is to demonstrate high-accuracy lunar landing capabilities, emphasizing the importance of landing precisely where intended on the lunar surface.

The launch occurred two weeks after India's successful lunar landing with its Chandrayaan-3 mission. SLIM is set to touch down near Mare Nectaris and will analyze the composition of rocks to gain insights into the moon's origin.

## Starship Fully Stacked

SpaceX is still gearing up for the second integrated test flight of its massive Starship rocket, and Ship 25 and Booster 9 currently sit fully stacked on the Orbital Launch Mount in Boca Chica, Texas. The first full-scale Starship test launch in April faced significant challenges, including multiple Raptor engine failures and a loss of control during the upper atmosphere phase, ultimately leading to an autonomous self-destruct sequence.

![](https://embed.filekitcdn.com/e/5MgHd6kkhZ8fZBAgpuZgWA/aBL3mB8cPKiP691T8ukeh)

SpaceX shared some previously unknown details [about the 4/20 launch in a recent blog post](https://www.spacex.com/updates/index.html), including that the "vehicle sustained fires from leaking propellant in the aft end of the Super Heavy booster, which eventually severed connection with the vehicle’s primary flight computer." However, SpaceX deemed the test flight a success as it provided valuable data for improving the rocket's performance.

The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) has completed its review of SpaceX’s Starship mishap report from the April test flight but has not yet granted approval for the next Starship flight. SpaceX must demonstrate that it has addressed 63 corrective actions, with a focus on those impacting public safety, before the FAA will consider granting a launch license modification for the upcoming mission.

[![](https://embed.filekitcdn.com/e/5MgHd6kkhZ8fZBAgpuZgWA/4S5DsUH833bHNoSvoJqdS4)](https://twitter.com/elonmusk/status/1700789411279966339)

Elon Musk shared a list of these corrective actions on social media, indicating that SpaceX has successfully completed 57 of them, with the remaining six applicable to future Starship flights. The FAA's oversight is designed to ensure that commercial launch operations meet safety and environmental standards.

SpaceX has made several enhancements and upgrades to the Starship rocket, addressing issues such as engine reliability, flight safety systems, and launch pad improvements. The next test flight will feature hot-staging separation, electric thrust vector controls, and stronger engine shielding, among other improvements, particularly around Stage 0 and the Orbital Launch Mount.

SpaceX still needs to apply for and receive a license modification from the FAA before getting the green light for the next Starship launch. The FAA will review SpaceX's progress before granting approval for the next Starship test flight, which could occur before the end of this month if all goes well.

## Atlas V Returns to Action

United Launch Alliance (ULA) successfully launched its Atlas V rocket (NROL-107), marking the end of a 10-month gap between launches, which was the longest in 20 years. The Atlas V, which has been a reliable workhorse in the launch industry, has a rich history of transporting payloads into space for various clients, including the military, the NRO, NASA, and commercial customers. The last Atlas V launch took place in November, marking the rocket's final flight from California. It has been nearly 11 months since the last Atlas V launch occurred at Cape Canaveral. United Launch Alliance is gradually transitioning away from the Atlas V in favor of its successor, the Vulcan rocket.

The NROL-107 launch went smoothly, with the rocket's upper stage deploying multiple satellites for the National Reconnaissance Office and the US Space Force into geosynchronous orbits, approximately 22,000 miles above the Earth's equator. These satellites will be used for surveillance and tracking other objects in space, particularly those in geosynchronous orbit, which is high above the Earth and offers a constant view of specific regions.

This launch ended the long hiatus for the Atlas V rocket, caused mainly due to customer delays. ULA has just one Delta IV Heavy left to launch slated for liftoff next year and 18 more Atlas V rockets left including seven designated for the long-delayed Boeing Starliner, nine for the Amazon Project Kuiper satellite constellation and two others.

The particular mission, called "Silent Barker," was postponed in late August due to weather concerns from Hurricane Idalia. The Silent Barker mission acts as a watchdog in space, continually monitoring the position of other objects in geosynchronous orbit to detect any potential threats to US satellites and will "deter aggression" by tracking satellites of potential adversaries in space.

In the future, additional Silent Barker satellites are planned to be launched to create a fully operational constellation by 2026, enhancing the ability to monitor and respond to potential threats in space.
